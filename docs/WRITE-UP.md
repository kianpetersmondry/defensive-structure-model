# How I Built a Model That Determines a Football Team's Defensive Structure

*Originally written in September 2026, when the project covered two matches (Morocco vs Spain and Morocco vs France). The model has grown since; see the [README](../README.md) and [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) for the current state.*

---

## Intro

Recently, a lot of modern teams have distinct playing styles when they are on Defense. Spains national recently won the world cup in 2026 and were known for how quickly they were able to pressure and win the ball back after losing it, allowing them to have really high possession in almost all of their games. In 2022, Morocco were the dark horse of the tournament, beating the likes of Portugal and Spain before they eventually fell to France. In these games, especially versus Spain, you could tell they were playing a really low line and having everyone (including the attackers back on defense, which is known as a low block. Now we know how to define this by simply watching the game and using the eye test to identify when teams are collectively pressing, or when they stay compact, or whether they have a high line or low one, but this project aims to identify these using tracking data and putting them in a computer model so we can analyze them further.

This will help to understand how different pressing styles actually work and can provide insight into how they can get broken down or how they hold up. In every defensive shape there is a trade-off. Push up to press the ball and you open space in behind. Sit deep to protect that space and you give the other team time on the ball. Thus, not only will this model aim to identify the style of play a team has, but also what space this team is leaving, and how teams can find a way to exploit it.

So I set out to build a model that could watch a full 90 minutes of tracking data, including every player's and the ball's position, and turn it into something a coach or an analyst could actually read: an animated recreation of the match with the defense's structure labeled in real time, the pressure on the ball carrier scored on the fly, and a live heatmap showing exactly where the defending team was leaving dangerous, reachable space. Not a highlight reel. Not a stat sheet. Something closer to film study with the tactical read already done for you. More analysis can and will be performed on this, but only once the sample size of videos is larger (currently only 2) and you can combine them to get useful information about defensive structures averaged over multiple teams and games.

I picked two matches to build it against, both Morocco, both from that same World Cup run: the Round of 16 win over Spain, and the semifinal loss to France. One is the signature defensive performance of the tournament; the other is the moment it finally gave way against a better attack. Together they're a useful first batch as it's the same team, the same defensive principles, tested against two very different opponents.

What follows is the story of actually building it, which doesn't contain a polished methods section, but is closer to a lab notebook. Some of my ideas worked on the first try. A lot of it didn't. I chased a heatmap bug for longer than I'd like to admit, threw out a metric I was sure would work, and rebuilt the classification logic more times than I can count once real match data kept finding edge cases my rules hadn't accounted for. This is that process, in roughly the order it happened.

## Process

### Data Collection and Initial Limitations

Before I could classify anything, I needed tracking data that worked rather than starting completely from scratch. Everything in this project runs on the PFF FC 2022 FIFA World Cup dataset — a free, request-based tracking dataset PFF makes available for research, covering all 64 matches of that tournament. For every match it provides, it tracks the x/y position of all 22 players and the ball, roughly 30 times a second. They put every game's information in a folder, and from it I pulled the two matches I wanted out of the 64: Morocco vs Spain (Round of 16) and Morocco vs France (the Semifinal).

The important caveat, and one worth saying plainly: this is broadcast-camera-derived tracking, not chip-in-the-ball or optical-tracking-rig data. It's inferred from video, and that shows. The first time I plotted it out, players and the ball would randomly teleport a few meters, freeze, or vanish for a stretch before reappearing somewhere else. This isn't unique to my two matches or something I did wrong pulling the data, but rather is a documented limitation of this kind of tracking. Roughly 27% of broadcast airtime across this dataset has no usable tracking at all, mostly because the ball is out of play, the broadcast cuts to a replay, or the camera goes to a close-up and loses the wider shot the tracking depends on. Feed that straight into a model, and it doesn't see a football match; it sees noise.

Some of it was fixable. Single-frame glitches, where a player's position spiked way off from where they were a tenth of a second earlier, and short gaps, could be cleaned up and bridged. But patched-up raw data still didn't move like real football, and players still slid around a little unnaturally frame to frame. That pushed me toward actual signal processing instead of quick patches: a Kalman filter paired with an RTS smoother, the standard approach for cleaning up noisy motion data by using not just where something is right now, but where it's been and where it's likely headed. This improved the raw data a lot, making it look smoother and not have teleportation problems, at least for the players.

The ball was the harder problem, and smoothing alone can't fix it. Players are large and stay in frame; the ball is small, fast, and constantly hidden behind bodies, so broadcast tracking loses it far more often, and no amount of filtering invents where the ball actually was during a gap that was never captured. So instead of smoothing my way out of it, I scanned the full match looking for the cleanest available stretch to find the best time to test my model on (before I extend it to the full game) and found the window with the highest percentage of frames where the ball was actually tracked, and the shortest gaps. The best I found was about 87% ball coverage, with a single worst gap of 8.5 seconds. Later, when I expanded the animation to cover full matches rather than just one hand-picked window, that same unevenness showed up everywhere; ball-tracking coverage across a match's chapters (which are usually around 5 minutes or so) ranges wildly from 10% to 90% of ball coverage depending on which stretch of the game you're looking at. I flag that on the chapter itself wherever it's especially rough, rather than hiding it.

Thus, we have a clear limitation with the model already due to being stuck with this free PFF FC tracking dataset, as I still do not have access to the advanced optical-tracking rig that professional companies and clubs probably use. If I did, I could probably swap in that data for my current one and run the rest of the model to get rid of these gaps, but for now this is just something we have to note down. An 8.5-second gap is a long time for a ball to be "missing" in something that's supposed to be reading exactly how a defense reacts to it, and a chapter with 10% ball coverage should be read with real skepticism about anything involving the ball specifically. Thus, I made sure to note these gaps very clearly, even within the model, as it will explicitly tell you when a gap is occurring. Since everything downstream, such as the phase classification, the pressure scoring, and the heatmap, will inherit whatever the tracking data hands it, gaps and all, it's at least worth being honest about where this model has its flaws before I built anything on top of it. There are still definitely a lot of minutes where the ball coverage is fine, and the model works as intended, so for now the focus will be on actually building the phase classification, the pressure scoring, and the heatmap, and testing how it looks on the parts that do have continuous and good coverage.

### Defining the Shape: Phases and the Back Line

The next problem was turning raw player positions into an actual read on what a team was doing defensively. Coaches and commentators talk about four basic defensive phases: Transition (the scramble right after losing the ball, before anyone's settled into a shape), High Press (pushing up to win the ball back quickly), Mid Block (holding a middle-of-the-pitch shape and inviting play toward it), and Low Block (dropping deep and defending close to your own goal). The eye test can spot these easily enough when watching live. Getting a computer to spot them from raw coordinates meant turning "the back line is high" into an actual number.

The number I settled on is how far a team's back line sits from its own goal, frame by frame. Above 40 meters, I call it a High Press. Between 32 and 40 meters, Mid Block. Below 32 meters, it's a candidate for Low Block, but only if the team is also compact, which means no more than 20 meters between the deepest and shallowest outfield player; otherwise it just gets downgraded back to Mid Block, since a team playing deep but stretched out isn't really holding a low block; it's just disorganized. Transition isn't a threshold at all; it's detected separately, by watching for the moment right after a turnover when a team's shape hasn't settled into a new value yet.

Alongside the phase label, I put a visual band directly on the pitch showing the team's actual length front-to-back, the gap between their deepest and most advanced outfield player, so you could see the shape driving the label, not just trust the label on faith.

### Scoring Pressure

A phase label tells you a team's general shape, but it doesn't tell you whether the ball carrier is actually under pressure right now. A Mid Block can be closing the ball down aggressively or just sitting there watching it; same shape, completely different situation. So the next part to work on was a pressure score: a number, recalculated every frame, for how much danger the ball carrier is actually in from nearby defenders.

The naive version of this is just "how close is the nearest defender," but proximity alone is misleading, a defender standing five meters away but facing the wrong direction, or slower than the attacker, isn't really a threat. What actually matters is whether a defender could get to the ball in time to affect the play. So instead of distance, I based the score on time-to-intercept: for each defender, using their position, speed, direction, and a fixed reaction time, I estimate how long it would take them to actually reach the ball carrier. A defender who could get there almost instantly contributes a lot of pressure; one who'd need several seconds contributes almost none. Every defender's contribution gets combined into one score per frame, so a ball carrier surrounded by three moderately threatening defenders reads as more pressured than one facing a single very threatening one.

To check whether the score actually meant something, I compared it against PFF's own analyst-tagged pressure events, moments a human watching the match had already flagged as "the ball carrier is under pressure here." On those tagged moments, the model's average score was 0.739; on everything else, 0.538. That gap is the model agreeing with a human's eye test often enough to trust it.

Once the pressure score existed, an obvious follow-up question came up: is a team actually engaging the ball, or just holding its shape and letting the game come to it? Those look identical on a phase label but are tactically very different. So I split all the phase labels to include weather its engaged or passive using the same pressure score, with a cutoff chosen to best separate the tagged-pressure moments from the rest. For the Morocco-Spain match this held up well. For Morocco-France, the same approach came out with noticeably weaker separation between engaged and passive moments, and I haven't dug into why yet, it's a real limitation of comparing this tag across the two matches, not something I've smoothed over. For more into this topic I noted down the initial test I did for this in the Morocco vs Spain game in the companion analysis section of the website. Or click this link: https://defensive-structure-model.netlify.app/companions/engaged-vs-passive-defense

### The Danger Heatmap

Phases and pressure both describe the defense itself. What I wanted next was a way to describe the other side of the trade-off: not just what shape a team is holding, but what space that shape is actually leaving open, and how dangerous that space is if the ball gets there.

"Open space" on its own isn't that useful, there's open space in the corner flag too, and nobody cares about that as its usually not that threatening to the goal. What matters is space that's both open and reachable, close enough to goal or positioned well enough that a realistic pass could put the ball there and cause a problem. That combination is what a model called Dangerous Accessible Space (DAS) measures, a way of rasterizing the whole pitch into a live heatmap, at any given moment, showing where the team in possession could realistically move the ball with one pass and how dangerous that spot would be if they did. I built this in as a real-time overlay on the animation, tinted to the possessing team's color, so you can watch it shift frame by frame as the defense's shape changes.

The first version I shipped looked wrong. Visually, it came out messy and hard to read, more like blotchy static than a smooth gradient. It turned out to be two separate bugs stacked on top of each other, not one. The first was a checkerboard pattern in the raw grid, and the space calculation was built on rays radiating outward from the ball at a fixed number of angles, and any pitch cell that happened to fall between two rays got left at zero even though there was real space there, which showed up as an alternating pattern of lit and dark cells. The second was a normalization problem: the actual underlying values varied by an enormous range, spanning something like eight orders of magnitude from the quietest cells to the hottest ones, and the way I was scaling that range into brightness caused almost every real hot spot to instantly clip to full brightness. That's why the first version looked like a flat, hard-edged block covering half the pitch instead of a graded glow centered on the danger.

The fix for both was to stop treating the raw numbers as something to display directly. I blurred each frame's raw grid slightly to smooth over the gaps between rays, and switched the brightness scaling to a logarithmic transform instead of a straight linear one, since that's the right way to compress a heavy-tailed range like this into something a human eye can actually read gradations in. Once both were in, I re-checked it against situations where I already knew what the answer should look like — a ball trapped in a crowd of defenders should show almost no glow around it, since there's nowhere safe to pass; an open ball carrier with time and space should show a real, visible bloom. The fixed version finally matched that intuition. For more on this topic, I once again noted down the initial test I did for this in the Morocco vs. Spain game in the companion analysis section of the website. Or click this link: https://defensive-structure-model.netlify.app/companions/space-in-behind

### Scaling to a Full Match

Everything up to this point was built and tested on that one hand-picked 5-minute window that had good ball coverage from the Morocco vs. Spain game, the cleanest stretch of tracking data I could find. That was the right call for building and debugging the phases, pressure score, and heatmap without the added noise of a bad tracking window getting in the way. But a 5-minute window isn't a match, and the whole point of this project was to read a full 90 minutes of defensive behavior, not one clip.

Extending it turned out to hit a wall I hadn't planned for: file size. At the same quality I'd built everything at, full frame-by-frame player positions, a heatmap resampled several times a second, a full match's worth of data added up to roughly 275MB for the animation alone and another 90MB for the heatmap. The tool I publish these as pages with has a hard 16MB limit per page, so I was over that limit by more than 10x, not by a little. I looked into whether I could stream the data on demand instead of loading it all at once, which would have sidestepped the size limit entirely, but that wasn't an option available to me. So the real choice was between dropping the quality (lower frame rate, a coarser heatmap), narrowing the scope (only showing the heatmap during a highlighted stretch instead of the whole match), or keeping full quality and splitting the match into multiple pages.

I went with splitting it into pages, at full quality, the same way a book gets split into chapters instead of shrinking the font to fit it on fewer pages. Each match's four periods (first half, second half, and two extra-time periods when and if they went to overtime) got divided into roughly 5-minute chapters, which came out to 26 chapters for the full Morocco vs Spain match. I thought this might also be good too because now you can pick out a specific moment of the match and easily pick out the 5 minute chapter of where it happened, rather than scrolling through a 90 or in this case 120 minute game.

Going from a single 5-minute window to a full match with extra time surfaced two real bugs that had been sitting there invisible the whole time, simply because the original window never exercised those parts of the data. The match clock had been calculated assuming every period lasts 45 minutes, which is wrong once you get into extra time, periods three and four run on their own separate clocks (about 15 minutes), so the displayed time was drifting off almost as soon as regulation ended. And a lookup used to match a player's ID to their real name and position broke on players who hadn't appeared in the original 5-minute window, mostly substitutes, showing a placeholder number instead of a real name. Both were exactly the kind of bug that a narrow test window hides and a full match forces into the open so I had to fix that now as well.

Once the full match existed as a set of chapter pages, the natural next step was making them easy to actually get to. What started as a flat list of 26 links grouped by period turned into a proper two-step picker, pick a match, then pick a chapter, and from there into an actual homepage: a hero section up top, the chapter picker, and a grid of companion analyses below it, so the whole thing could read as a real site you'd send someone a link to, rather than a folder of separate pages.

### Checking Pressing Intensity: PPDA

Everything so far was built from scratch, using raw player and ball positions. But soccer analytics already has an established stat for pressing intensity that doesn't need tracking data at all: PPDA, or passes allowed per defensive action — basically, how many passes does the other team get to complete, on average, before you make a tackle, interception, or foul. A low number means you're pressing them constantly and not letting them settle; a high number means you're mostly letting them have the ball and waiting.

I built a chart plotting PPDA for both teams across the entire match, including extra time, in 5-minute windows, alongside every turnover in the game. For Morocco vs Spain, the numbers told almost exactly the story you'd expect from watching the game: Morocco's PPDA came out to 18.44, extremely passive, letting Spain string together long spells of possession before ever really contesting it, while Spain's PPDA was 4.04, aggressively pressing at almost every opportunity. That's a completely independent measurement from anything tracking-based, and it matched the eye test for that match closely.

That gave me an obvious way to check my own work: if the phase classifier is actually measuring something real, PPDA should get lower (more aggressive) as a team's classified phase shifts from Low Block to Mid Block to High Press, since that's exactly what pressing theory predicts. The first version of this comparison didn't line up cleanly, and chasing that down turned up a real bug, not a flaw in the theory. The timestamp I was using to line up PPDA windows against the phase data was based on frame count assuming a fixed frame rate for the whole match, not the actual match clock, and it drifted further out of sync the longer the match went, off by as much as 12.5 minutes by the time extra time rolled around. Once I recomputed everything on the corrected clock, and restricted the comparison to time each team was actually in an organized defensive shape (excluding transition and on-the-ball moments, which don't really have a "pressing intensity" the same way), PPDA rose cleanly from High Press to Mid Block to Low Block for both teams, exactly as pressing theory predicts. That was a genuinely useful check: two totally different ways of measuring the same idea, one from raw tracking data and one from a decades-old passing stat, agreeing once both were done correctly. You can see the full PPDA breakdown and this comparison for yourself in the companion analysis section of the website, or with this link: https://defensive-structure-model.netlify.app/companions/ppda-vs-phase-detector

### Other Ideas for the Model

Once phases, pressure, and the heatmap were all working, a natural question came up: were there other measurements worth adding to make the read even sharper? I ended up with three candidate ideas at once. One became the Engaged/Passive tag already covered above. The other two are worth telling separately, because one got tested seriously and rejected, and the other never made it past being an idea.

The first of the two is something I called Ball-Proximal Compactness, or BPC, instead of measuring how spread out the whole team is, it measures how spread out just the five players closest to the ball are at any given moment. The pitch is big, and a team can look loosely organized overall while the group actually around the ball is marking tightly, or vice versa, so this felt like it might be a genuinely useful third variable alongside back-line depth and team compactness for defining a team's structure. Rather than just adding it because it sounded reasonable, I tested it properly: I checked whether tight or loose ball-proximal marking actually predicted real turnovers happening a few seconds later, using the actual events in the match rather than my own intuition. The result was that it didn't hold up as a defining variable, there was no clean threshold where "tighter than this" reliably meant something, and once you already know the team's overall compactness, knowing this extra number didn't add any real predictive value on top of it. Rather than throw the whole idea out, I kept it as a simpler descriptive tag, Tight or Loose, that shows up next to the phase label without pretending to be one of the core defining variables. It's an honest downgrade from what I originally hoped it would be, but it's still a useful thing to glance at. For more about this view the companion analysis for it, or use the link: https://defensive-structure-model.netlify.app/companions/ball-proximal-compactness

The second idea never got built at all, and I want to be upfront about that rather than only talking about things that shipped. It came from a coaching note my professor mentioned to me, arguing that a team's back-line height in the moments right after a play often follows from something specific: whether there's genuine, engaging pressure on the ball-side wide player, not just a general team-wide rule about how high or low to play. That's a real, testable claim against data I already had, since I already measure both pressure and back-line height. But at the point this came up, the priority shifted toward cleaning up the visuals across what I'd already built and adding a second match, so this idea got explicitly set aside rather than pursued. It's a real gap in this version of the project, and a legitimate next thing to test if I come back to it.

### A Second Match

With Morocco vs Spain fully working, or at least me being content with how it looks, the next step was proving the whole pipeline wasn't a one-off built around a single game's quirks. I picked Morocco vs France, the semifinal, as the second match: same team, same defensive principles, but tested against a better attack, and France won that one 2-0, so the story of the data itself is different: no extra time, just two periods, first half and second half, which split into 20 chapters instead of 26.

I could have just copy-pasted the whole pipeline and swapped in Morocco vs Spain's names for Morocco vs France's, but that would have meant maintaining two nearly-identical copies of everything forever, and any future fix would need to be made twice. Instead, I made the pipeline match-aware: one shared codebase that reads which match it's building from a single setting, and looks up that match's team names, colors, and file locations from one place instead of having them hardcoded all over the code. Add a new match, and the rest of the pipeline doesn't need to be touched; just run.

Building it this way also caught a real, honest difference between the two matches' data instead of letting it slide by unnoticed. The pressure score depends on how fast each player can realistically move, which I originally calculated per player from their own tracked top speed in the Morocco-Spain data. Morocco vs. France's tracking data doesn't include a speed field for players at all, so there was nothing to calculate that from. The pipeline already had a fallback built in for exactly this kind of gap: a single assumed max speed used for every player instead of their own, so it didn't break, but it does mean the pressure scores for this match are built on a less precise assumption than the first match's were. That's a real difference in data quality between the two games, not a bug I fixed, and it's worth keeping in mind if anyone ever compares pressure numbers across the two matches directly. It will, however, be fixed before I start adding more games to this project.

The second match also came with a more mixed result on the Engaged/Passive read, mentioned earlier: the same method that worked well distinguishing engaged from passive defense in the Spain game came out noticeably weaker on the France game. I haven't dug into why, and I'm not pretending it's solved. It's flagged as an open question rather than quietly ignored.

One thing I chose not to do yet: the five companion analysis charts (the pressing-intensity chart, the space-behind-the-line chart, and the others) are still built for Morocco vs Spain only. Extending each of those to a second match is real additional work, and my plan for now is to wait until I have more matches in the pipeline and build one combined cross-match view instead of rebuilding each chart per match one at a time.

### What's Next

Two matches is a start, not a conclusion. Everything in this project so far describes exactly two games from one tournament, and the honest answer to "does this generalize" is that I don't know yet. The next real milestone isn't a new feature, it's more data: enough matches that patterns can be compared across teams and games instead of read off a sample size of one. A third match is a much smaller lift now than the second one was, since the pipeline is already built to take a new match without being rewritten, but the plan is to build toward roughly five games before trying any real cross-match analysis, rather than drawing conclusions too early from too little.

The other real limitation is the one I flagged at the very start: this whole project runs on broadcast-camera tracking with real gaps in it, especially for the ball. That's fine for building and testing the model, but it's not the ceiling I want to build toward long-term. The actual fix is custom tracking pulled directly from match footage instead of relying on someone else's broadcast-derived dataset, which would mean far fewer gaps and, likely, a lot more matches to choose from since I wouldn't be limited to whatever's already been packaged and released. That's a real infrastructure project on its own, and it's the next thing on my list once I have footage to build it from.

There's also a smaller, more specific thing worth revisiting: the coaching-note idea about wide-side pressure predicting back-line height, which I set aside earlier rather than dropped for good. It's still sitting there as a genuinely testable question against data I already have.

None of this makes the current version useless, it does exactly what I set out to build: it watches a match, labels the defensive shape frame by frame, scores the pressure on the ball, and shows the space being left behind, all together, in one place, for two real World Cup matches anyone can watch and check against the eye test. It's a working prototype with its limitations stated up front rather than hidden, which is exactly what a first version of something like this should be. The rest is a matter of more data, better source tracking, and time.
