Reading this

Each frame classifies the out-of-possession team into Transition, High Press, Mid Block or Low Block, from defensive-line depth and inter-line compactness (thresholds from published pressing-height benchmarks).

The small ENGAGED/PASSIVE tag next to each phase is an add-on, not a new phase: a deep, compact line only means real pressure is being applied if someone is actually closing the ball down, so this reads the same pressure-on-ball-carrier signal (smoothed over 3s, like depth and inter-line already are) and flags whether that's genuinely happening right now. It's validated against PFF's own analyst-tagged pressure events, not eyeballed — and it's descriptive, not a replacement for the phase label: every phase, for both teams, turns out to be a real mix of both (see the “Engaged vs. Passive Defense” companion analysis for the full breakdown).

The TIGHT/LOOSE tag is a second, independent add-on: whole-team inter-line is the span from deepest defender to furthest attacker, but Forcher et al. found it's the compactness of just the 5 players nearest the ball — not the whole team — that actually predicts defensive success. This tag reads that ball-proximal group directly (max distance between any two of them, smoothed the same way), so it can flag a loosely-marked ball carrier even inside a phase whose whole-team shape looks compact, or vice versa. It's genuinely different information from inter-line (r≈0.46, not a restatement) — but it turned out not to reliably predict winning the ball back sooner, so rather than fold it into the phase thresholds themselves (where a wrong cutoff would misclassify real phases), it's kept here as a descriptive read on ball-side marking quality, same treatment as Engaged/Passive.

The pressure ring is a time-to-intercept estimate per nearby defender — reaction time, current velocity, max speed — combined into one 0–100% score on the carrier, in the spirit of Bekkers (2025).

Team shape (depth, inter-line, gap) is tracked every frame regardless of who has the ball. Pressure and “on the ball” need a resolved carrier, so they briefly go blank during loose-ball moments — a real feature of broken play, not missing data.

The danger heatmap is Dangerous Accessible Space (Bischofberger & Baca, 2026) — a physics-based pass-completion simulation combined with a fitted distance-and-angle danger model — rasterized across the whole pitch and resampled at 5Hz, showing exactly where the team in possession could access dangerous space with one realistic pass right now. It's the same model behind the “Space in Behind” companion chart, just read spatially instead of as a time series.

Brightness is log-scaled, not linear — raw danger values span several orders of magnitude between a tightly marked ball and a wide-open outlet pass, so a straight 0-to-max scale would make almost everything look either off or maxed-out. The glow is naturally strongest right around the ball and tapers outward, since danger here means reachable right now with one pass; it goes faint or disappears entirely when the carrier is closely marked in a crowd, which is a real reading (little safe, dangerous space to find), not a rendering gap.
